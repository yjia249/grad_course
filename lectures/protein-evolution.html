Lecture 15

Protein Evolution

Epistasis, fitness landscapes, and emergent evolutionary time scales from inverse statistical physics

Back to course homepage

Paper Information

Di Bari, Bisardi, Cotogno, Weigt, and Zamponi (2024), Emergent time scales of epistasis in protein evolution, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 121, e2406807121.

DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2406807121

Why this is an excellent final lecture paper: it is exactly the kind of work that makes statistical physics feel indispensable to biophysics. The paper builds a data-driven model of protein evolution in which sequence change happens through random nucleotide mutations, insertions, and deletions, while selection acts through a learned fitness landscape Definition: A map assigning higher or lower evolutionary success to different sequences. Why it matters: It is the object that turns protein evolution into a statistical-physics problem on a huge discrete configuration space. inferred from family sequence statistics. This lets the authors connect short laboratory time scales, intermediate in silico trajectories, and long natural divergence within one coherent model.

Big question: How does epistasis Definition: Dependence of the effect of one mutation on the surrounding sequence context. Why it matters: Epistasis is the reason protein evolution cannot be understood as independent mutations at independent sites. reshape protein evolution across time, and why do mutational effects change slowly and collectively as sequences diverge instead of being controlled only by a few obvious pairwise clashes?

Where This Lecture Sits in Protein Evolution

Molecular evolution side

Main concern: How mutations arise, fix, reverse, and diversify proteins across natural lineages.

Why important here: The paper directly addresses contingency, entrenchment, and the predictability of evolutionary trajectories.

Protein biophysics side

Main concern: How sequence determines stability, function, constraints, and mutational tolerance.

Why important here: Evolutionary dynamics is filtered through protein-level constraints encoded in the inferred landscape.

Statistical physics side

Main concern: How to infer effective interactions, landscapes, and slow collective dynamics in a very high-dimensional system.

Why important here: The paper uses inverse statistical physics and stochastic sampling as the core modeling language.

Why end the course here?

Reason: This topic brings together sequence ensembles, landscapes, stochastic dynamics, collective effects, and experimentally measurable mutational phenotypes.

Teaching value: It is a clean capstone for showing how soft-matter and statistical-physics ideas can illuminate living molecular systems.

History of This Topic

Classical population genetics era. Early theory established mutation, selection, drift, and fixation as the core language of evolution, but usually at a coarse level without explicit high-dimensional protein landscapes.
Protein sequence era. As large sequence databases grew, researchers began comparing homologs to infer conservation, variability, and functional constraints across protein families.
Epistasis becomes central. Work on historical contingency and mutational interactions showed that the effect of a mutation depends strongly on the sequence background, making independent-site pictures inadequate.
Inverse statistical physics enters biology. Direct coupling analysis and Potts-like models turned multiple-sequence alignments into effective interaction models that could capture pairwise constraints, structural contacts, and mutational effects.
Deep mutational scanning changes the data landscape. High-throughput experiments made it possible to measure the effects of thousands of mutations at once, revealing how mutational landscapes shift across homologs and backgrounds.
Directed evolution and laboratory evolution bridge time scales. Experimental evolution can probe short mutational trajectories, but long natural divergence remains much harder to observe directly.
2024: what this paper adds. Di Bari et al. build one epistatic evolutionary model that reproduces both short-term experimental evolution and long-term natural family statistics, then use it to identify a slow emergent time scale of epistatic context change.
Why this matters now. The work speaks to forecasting evolution, interpreting deep mutational scans, understanding antibiotic-resistance proteins, and deciding when simpler phylogenetic or independent-site models break down.

How the Methods Evolved

Comparative sequence analysis

Old foundation: Align homologous proteins and ask which positions are conserved or variable.

Limitation: Conservation alone cannot explain context dependence of mutation effects.

Deep mutational scanning

Upgrade: Measure fitness or function for thousands of mutations in parallel.

Why important: DMS made mutational landscapes directly observable and showed that they shift with sequence background.

Directed evolution

Upgrade: Mutate proteins experimentally and select for function over a few evolutionary rounds.

Why important: It gives short-time trajectories that a generative model can be tested against.

Potts / DCA inference

Main idea: Infer effective couplings between sites from sequence covariation in a protein family.

Why important: This is the inverse-statistical-physics step that turns an alignment into a quantitative fitness surrogate.

bmDCA

Main idea: Use Boltzmann-machine learning to infer the generative model of the protein family.

Why important: It is the technical engine behind the landscape used in the paper.

MCMC evolutionary dynamics

Main idea: Evolve sequences stochastically with proposed mutations accepted or rejected according to the inferred landscape.

Why important: This lets the authors connect molecular mutation processes to emergent long-time evolutionary behavior.

Entropy and mutability diagnostics

Main idea: Use site entropy and related observables to classify conserved, mutable, and epistatic positions.

Why important: It provides a clean bridge from raw sequence statistics to physical interpretation.

Contingency and entrenchment analysis

Main idea: Measure how mutational gains change before and after a mutation occurs along a trajectory.

Why important: These observables quantify epistasis dynamically rather than only statically.

Main Paper: Modeling and Analysis Workflow

1. Start from a protein family alignment

System: The paper focuses on the beta-lactamase family and uses natural homologs as the data source for learning constraints.

Reason: Family-level variation contains the information needed to infer the effective landscape.

2. Infer an epistatic fitness surrogate

Tool: bmDCA learns a Potts-like generative model whose energy acts as a proxy for evolutionary fitness.

Reason: This is the key statistical-physics step: infer interactions, then interpret evolution as dynamics on the inferred landscape.

3. Simulate sequence evolution

Dynamics: Propose random nucleotide mutations, insertions, and deletions, then filter them through selection using the inferred landscape.

Reason: The authors want a biologically motivated mutational process, not only a static model of sequence statistics.

4. Validate short-time behavior

Comparison: Match in silico results against laboratory evolution around TEM-1 and PSE-1 beta-lactamase wild types.

Reason: This checks whether the inferred family landscape can predict local mutational variability near specific wild-type backgrounds.

5. Validate long-time behavior

Comparison: Check whether simulated divergence reproduces the broad statistics of the natural protein family.

Reason: The model must work not only near one wild type but across long evolutionary distances.

6. Classify site types

Outputs: Conserved, mutable, and strongly context-dependent epistatic sites are separated using local and global variability diagnostics.

Reason: This makes the collective nature of epistasis visible position by position.

7. Measure contingency and entrenchment

Question: How does the value of a mutation depend on what happened before it, and how hard does it become to revert after the background adapts around it?

Reason: These are dynamic signatures of epistasis that students can connect to evolutionary history.

8. Expose the emergent slow time scale

Main claim: Mutations happen quickly within a given context, but the sequence context itself changes only slowly through many collective epistatic effects.

Reason: This is the central take-home message and the clearest statistical-physics insight of the paper.

What to Calculate First

  • DCA energy difference \(\Delta E\)Definition: Change in inferred landscape energy caused by a mutation. Why first: It is the paper's basic mutational effect proxy and the shortest route from statistical inference to evolutionary selection.: Start by asking how one mutation shifts the sequence on the inferred landscape.
  • Site entropyDefinition: Variability of amino-acid occupancy at one position. Why first: It gives an intuitive measure of mutability and is one of the first validation observables in the paper.: Compare local site entropy from simulations against experimental and family data.
  • Sequence divergenceDefinition: Distance from the starting sequence, often summarized by normalized Hamming distance. Why first: It is the natural clock for asking when epistasis changes mutational effects.: Use divergence as the horizontal axis for predictability loss.
  • Contingency and entrenchment curvesDefinition: Time-dependent gain or loss in mutational favorability before and after a mutation occurs. Why first: These curves make dynamic epistasis visible in one plot.: They are the clearest way to teach how context evolves around a mutation.
  • DMS correlation across backgroundsDefinition: Correlation of mutational-effect measurements between wild type and diverged homologs. Why first: It shows when evolutionary forecasting starts to fail because context has drifted too far.: This is where the paper connects directly to experiment.

Main Teaching Points

  • This paper shows how to turn protein evolution into a stochastic dynamics problem on an inferred high-dimensional landscape.
  • The strongest message is not only that epistasis exists, but that much of it is collective and unfolds on a slower time scale than individual mutational events.
  • Inverse statistical physics is useful here because it converts sequence ensembles into an effective generative model that can be simulated, tested, and interpreted.
  • Short-term laboratory evolution and long-term natural divergence do not need separate conceptual frameworks if the underlying landscape model is good enough.
  • The paper is especially powerful for teaching because it links observables students can compute, entropy, divergence, contingency, DMS correlation, to broad evolutionary ideas.

Model Comparison

Independent-site models

Best for: Quick baseline estimates of conservation and mutability.

Limitation: They miss epistasis and cannot capture context-dependent mutational effects.

Potts / DCA models

Best for: Capturing pairwise effective couplings and reproducing family statistics.

Limitation: The inferred couplings are effective and do not always map one-to-one onto physical contacts or mechanisms.

Phylogenetic substitution models

Best for: Tree-based sequence evolution and large-scale comparative inference.

Limitation: Many standard versions use simplified site independence assumptions that weaken epistasis predictions.

DMS-only empirical models

Best for: High-resolution local fitness maps around one or a few backgrounds.

Limitation: They do not automatically tell you how mutational effects drift over much longer evolutionary distances.

Laboratory directed evolution

Best for: Real short-time adaptive trajectories under controlled selection.

Limitation: It is experimentally expensive and usually cannot span the divergence of natural families.

This paper's hybrid framework

Best for: Bridging local experiments and long-time family statistics within one epistatic model.

Limitation: It still depends on model assumptions about how inferred energy relates to selection and how mutations are proposed.

Concept Glossary (Quick Lookup)

Epistasis

Definition: Dependence of one mutation's effect on other residues in the sequence.

Why important: It is the core phenomenon the paper quantifies across time scales.

Fitness landscape

Definition: Effective map from sequence to evolutionary favorability.

Why important: It is the object inferred from sequence data and then explored dynamically.

bmDCA

Definition: Boltzmann-machine direct coupling analysis for learning a generative model of a protein family.

Why important: It provides the inferred energy function that drives the simulations.

Potts model

Definition: A multi-state interacting-site model often used to describe amino-acid couplings in proteins.

Why important: It is the statistical-physics backbone behind many modern sequence-inference methods.

Contingency

Definition: A mutation becomes favorable because earlier substitutions prepared the background for it.

Why important: It shows how history constrains future accessibility.

Entrenchment

Definition: After a mutation occurs, later substitutions make reversion increasingly costly.

Why important: It is the forward-time partner of contingency and an important signal of dynamic epistasis.

Deep mutational scanning

Definition: High-throughput assay measuring the effects of many mutations in parallel.

Why important: It provides the experimental data type most directly connected to the paper's predictability tests.

Emergent time scale

Definition: A slow collective time scale not obvious from the fast microscopic mutation steps alone.

Why important: It is the main conceptual contribution of the paper.
